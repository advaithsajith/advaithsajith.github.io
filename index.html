V · Film & fashion

Every thread is a line of code.

Why is there a ballerina on a computer scientist’s page? Because a dress, a dance and a program are all built the same way: small rules, repeated precisely, until something beautiful moves. The weave decides how a fabric falls; the physics decides how a dancer turns; the code decides both here.

The physics of a pirouette

A turn starts with torque: her foot pushes against the floor, and that twisting force gives her angular momentum, L = Iω. I is her moment of inertia (how far her mass sits from the spin axis) and ω is how fast she turns.

With no outside torque, L stays the same. So when she pulls her arms in, I falls and ω rises: try the button and watch the numbers. The floor and the air add a small opposite torque, so L leaks away and she stops. A fouetté, the whipping leg in Swan Lake, is a dancer adding a kick of torque on every turn.

Her skirt flares because each bit of fabric wants to fly outward: tan θ = ω²r / g. Light, soft fabrics lift at once; heavy or stiff ones barely move. Change the fabric and spin her again.

In 1804 the Jacquard loom wove patterns from punched cards, the idea that led to the first programmable machines. In FIRST LEGO League my team rebuilt a loom from LEGO.

IX · The type

Good design is mostly invisible spacing.

Writing, code and clothes all come down to spacing: where a sentence breaks, how a function is laid out, how a hem falls. Typographers call the space between two letters kerning, and readers only notice it when it goes wrong. The letters on this page are drawn with their guide lines showing.
